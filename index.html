Methodology, appreciation & return framing →

Also not shown: tax benefits like depreciation, which can meaningfully boost real returns for many investors — but depend entirely on your individual situation, so that's a conversation for your CPA, not this table.

On appreciation: The appreciation rate is my underwriting assumption for that specific submarket—not a historical market average or a guarantee. It's set deliberately at the low end of what a long-term hold there should average, not a best-case number. It's still a forecast, not a closed transaction, which is why it's marked in blue everywhere it shows up below.

One more framing note: Modeled Return is labeled "this year," but two of its three ingredients — appreciation and the vacancy already built into rent — are long-run averages, not a literal trailing-12-month report. That's on purpose: any single real year is too noisy to underwrite from (one vacant month or one hot comp can swing it), so the stabilized long-run figure is the more honest input for a snapshot. For closed properties, cash flow and principal paydown are pulled from the property's actual year; for pipeline properties, those operating figures are underwritten.

Why does the return percentage get smaller over time? →

Because early on, you're using a lot of the bank's money and relatively little of your own.

Imagine you put $50,000 into a $250,000 house. If the house goes up 4%, that's a $10,000 increase in value.

Early on, that $10,000 is large compared with the $50,000 of your own money you started with — about a 20% gain relative to that starting amount.

Early: $10,000 ÷ $50,000 = 20%

Over time, you pay down the mortgage and own more of the house yourself. That ownership is called equity.

Equity is the part of the house you actually own — roughly the home's value minus what you still owe on the mortgage.

Years later, imagine you now have about $200,000 of equity in the house. If the house rises by that same $10,000, the gain is now only about 5% relative to the $200,000 you own.

Later: $10,000 ÷ $200,000 = 5%
The house didn't perform worse. You simply own much more of it, so borrowed money is giving your return less of a boost. That boost from using borrowed money is what investors mean by leverage.

That's what the downward curve below is illustrating.

What exactly is this chart comparing?

The chart uses CAGR, which is just a way of turning a multi-year result into one average yearly growth rate. Think of it as:

"What yearly return would have gotten me from where I started to where I ended?"

For this comparison, money produced along the way is assumed to be reinvested rather than spent.

The S&P 500 line uses a 9% assumed long-term annual total return, with dividends reinvested. The rental-property model similarly assumes cash generated by the investment is reinvested back into the same investment strategy.

The 9% isn't a prediction, and it doesn't mean the S&P 500 earns 9% every year. It's simply the benchmark used in this illustration.

Questions Investors Ask

What's the criteria for picking these properties — what do they all have in common?

I don't start with a house. I start with the market, then the submarket, then the neighborhood, the community, and only then the property, layout, lot and finishes.

I'm looking for the same basic combination every time: a 3–4 bedroom single-family home or duplex, usually new construction from an established builder, in an affordable part of a large and economically diversified metro. I want population and job growth, a rental market with real tenant depth, good schools, manageable new supply, landlord-friendly rules, and property managers I'd be comfortable trusting with my own house. I want a layout with as broad as possible of an appeal, and a lot with no big flaws.

But checking boxes isn't enough. The numbers still have to work.

A beautiful house in a great market doesn't make the cut if the rent is too weak for the price. A high-cash-flow property doesn't make the cut if I think the neighborhood, supply picture, builder, or tenant base creates too much long-term risk.

The important part of the process is actually what gets rejected. I look at far more properties than I recommend, usually dozens for every one selected. When there is nothing I like that is available I will let you know that too.

How do you know these markets so well if you don't live in them?

I don't think research from a laptop is enough to tell someone else where to put $50,000–$100,000.

I only recommend markets where I am personally invested. I manage my own rentals directly, including leasing, which means I experience the tenant market firsthand: what applicants ask for, which neighborhoods they respond to, what rent actually gets attention, how seasonality changes demand, and how long a house really takes to lease.

That operating experience sits on top of the research: employment growth, employers moving in or out, infrastructure, development plans, construction permits, schools, rents, property taxes, insurance, housing supply and everything else that can change the long-term economics of the submarket.

And there's an important order to this: I become comfortable putting my own money into a market before I become comfortable recommending that market to somebody else.

That doesn't mean I can't be wrong. It means when I recommend a market, I'm exposed to many of the same things you are — and I'm learning from the same tenant pool with my own properties.

How do I know these numbers are real?

Fair question — I'd ask it too. Chances are you got to me through someone who already owns a property on this list. Ask them to find their deal by purchase price and state and check their numbers against these.

Please feel free to ask for documentation on any other number. I'm happy to share anything of my own and most other investors are happy to share theirs as well - our accumulated experience base helps us all.

Were there bad deals? Disappointments? Misses?

Absolutely. This isn't a highlight reel.

One property rented for $25 a month under my low estimate. Two sat vacant for two full months. One had a hidden electric sewage pump — found out about it when I paid $4,325 to replace it at the two-year mark. That one still stings a little. One lost a tenant at month three and had to go through a second vacancy in the same year.

These are the outliers, not the pattern — and I work hard to keep the surprises on the happy side. None of them were bad enough to break the underlying logic of the deal. Every single miss taught me something that's made the next deal sharper.

Why is this heavily clustered in Arkansas?

The concentration isn't intentional. Right now, Arkansas is simply where I'm finding the best combination of purchase price, achievable rent, growth trajectory, property taxes, builder incentives and manageable supply. You can see the result of that in the cash-flow and cash-on-cash numbers in the table.

That can change quickly. Rent being too low relative to price is the most common reason I reject a market or property. I've backed away from parts of Raleigh because I didn't like the amount of new supply coming in, and Texas property taxes make it much harder for a deal there to cash flow. Even within a market I like, I'll reject individual houses because of the layout or a particular lot.

So I'm not trying to build an Arkansas portfolio. Arkansas is just where the filter has been producing the most interesting properties lately. If that changes, the next purchases will change with it.

Why is vacancy estimated at 30 days?

Thirty days isn't a promise. It's an underwriting standard.

When I give you a projected rent, I'm deliberately trying to choose a number at which I believe a good tenant should be obtainable within about 30 days, rather than showing you the highest rent I can find on Zillow and assuming somebody will eventually pay it.

The real test is what happened afterward.

Most of the properties on this page have either leased in less than 30 days, rented for more than I originally projected, or both. Some haven't — two took about two months, and those misses included in the record too.

What does the process actually look like?

The transaction usually runs: sourcing → financing → inspection → closing → property management → tenant → accounting → taxes → repairs.

My formal role is your buyer's agent through closing. After closing, the legal transaction is finished — but owning the investment obviously isn't.

I'll help you get through the handoff to property management, tenant placement, rent decisions and the other unfamiliar pieces until you feel comfortable. Existing clients also continue to contact me about renewals, managers, repairs, rents and future purchases, and I'm happy to remain a resource.

How do you represent me, and how do you get paid?

When you hire me to represent you, I'm your buyer's agent under a written representation agreement for that transaction.

Before you sign it, you'll see exactly what I'm being paid, who is expected to pay it, what I owe you, how long the agreement lasts, and how either of us can end the relationship.

The exact legal duties vary somewhat by state and by the agreement we're using, but the basic principle is simple: I'm representing you, not the builder.

That includes duties such as confidentiality, disclosure of material information, reasonable care, following your lawful instructions and putting your interests ahead of my own within the agency relationship.

There is also an economic reality worth saying out loud: I normally get paid when you buy a property. That gives me a financial interest in a transaction closing.

A fiduciary relationship doesn't make that incentive disappear. It means I have a duty not to let that incentive dictate the advice I give you, and am dead serious in performing it. Full stop.

In residential real estate right now, it's most common for the seller to pay commission for both agents — the one representing them and the one representing the buyer. With new construction, that means the builder typically covers my commission, not you. You'll always know upfront if that's the case and exactly how much it is.

I don't charge you anything on top of that, and I don't take money or gifts from anyone else I connect you with. If money is moving in relation to your deal, you'll know about it.

Why include appreciation in ROI when you don't actually know what it'll be?

Because leaving appreciation out completely would also give you an incomplete picture of leveraged real estate — but treating an appreciation estimate like cash flow would be misleading.

So I separate the two.

Cash flow and mortgage principal paydown are things we can observe. Future appreciation isn't. It's an assumption about what the property may be worth later.

I still model it because even relatively modest changes in the value of a leveraged property can materially affect the investor's long-term result. But I don't think you should buy a property simply because the appreciation assumption makes the total-return number look exciting.

When we're evaluating a deal, I want you to look at it at 0% appreciation first:

  • What does the property produce from rent?
  • How much principal is being paid down?
  • Are you still comfortable owning it if its value goes nowhere for several years?

Then we can look at appreciation as a range of possible outcomes rather than a promise.

That's also why appreciation is visually separated throughout this page. The estimate matters, but it doesn't deserve the same confidence as money that has actually been earned.

When is it clear I shouldn't buy an investment like this?

While my own personal financials are heavily reliant on these properties as long term investments, it's definitely not necessarily the right move for everyone. Some scenarios in which it certainly isn't:

  • If a single property would be more than something like 10% of your net worth. That's not diversified, and no investment here is risk-free.
  • If you're going to need that money back soon. Your down payment, and most of your return, is stuck in the house until you sell or refinance — and doing either one at the wrong time can cost you.
  • If you're counting on the monthly cash flow for anything essential. A late tenant or a vacancy can make that number go quiet, or negative, without much warning.
